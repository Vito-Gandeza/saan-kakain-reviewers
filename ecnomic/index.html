Start here

Money now and money later are the same money

Engineering economy splits into two halves. Knowing which half you are in tells you which tools are allowed.

Present economy is the half you are in now. Alternatives differ over a short horizon, so the time value of money is ignored. No interest rates, no discounting, no present-worth factors. A peso today and a peso six months from now are the same peso.

Time-value economy is the other half, later in the course. Alternatives span years, so interest, compounding, present worth, annual worth and rate of return all enter.

The dividing line in practice: if the study period is short enough that interest would not change the ranking, you are in present economy. Every problem in your set runs over months or days.

Two things you will not need

No compound interest. If you reach for \(P = F(1+i)^{-n}\) anywhere in this set, you have misread the problem.

No statistics. There is no probability, no distribution, no expected value, no regression anywhere in these thirteen problems. Every number given is treated as certain. If you are worried about half-remembered statistics, you can set that worry down — what this topic actually requires is covered in the two sections below.

Preliminaries · 1 of 2

The cost vocabulary the problems assume you have

These terms come from cost accounting, and the problem set uses them without defining them. The tag on each says whether that kind of cost normally enters a present-economy decision.

Fixed costDepends
Does not change with output volume — rent, supervisor salaries, depreciation, insurance. Fixed does not mean irrelevant. It is irrelevant only when it is the same under every alternative. In make-or-buy it usually continues after you stop making, which is the whole trap.
Variable costUsually relevant
Moves in proportion to output — direct materials, piece-rate labor, power. In Problem 6 the day-wage is variable with respect to man-days worked.
Direct costUsually relevant
Traceable to one unit of product without guesswork. Direct material and direct labor.
Indirect cost / overheadDepends
Shared across products, allocated rather than traced — factory power, maintenance, supervision. Splits into variable overhead (rises with output) and fixed overhead (does not). Problem 2 gives you both, separately, on purpose.
Sunk costNever relevant
Already spent and unrecoverable. It cannot be changed by any choice you make now, so it never belongs in a comparison. Money already poured into a design, a study, or a half-finished job.
Opportunity costOften relevant
The value of the best alternative you give up. It never appears in an accounting ledger but it is a real cost. In Problem 13 the paint you fail to produce with slower media is an opportunity cost.
Avoidable / unavoidable costThe make-or-buy key
Avoidable costs disappear if you drop the activity; unavoidable ones continue. Only avoidable costs belong in the “make” column of a make-or-buy comparison — and the unavoidable ones must be added to the “buy” column, because you pay them either way.
Differential (incremental) costThe decider
The difference in total cost between two alternatives. This is the only number that ever decides anything. Everything else in this vocabulary exists to help you work out which costs are differential.
Marginal costRelevant
The cost of producing one more unit. In a short-run decision it is normally the variable cost per unit, since fixed cost does not move.
Contribution marginRelevant
Selling price minus variable cost, per unit. What each unit sold contributes toward covering fixed cost and then producing profit. Problem 4 hands you ₱ 8 per unit already computed — do not subtract variable cost from it again.
Liquidated damagesRelevant
A penalty per day of late delivery, agreed in the contract in advance so neither side has to prove actual losses in court. A real, differential cost. Problem 6 turns entirely on it.
Economic advantageThe answer
The amount by which the chosen alternative beats the rejected one. Always report it with units and direction — “cheaper by ₱ 56,250 per month”, never a bare number.

Preliminaries · 2 of 2

The five pieces of arithmetic you actually use

1. Unit analysis — the error-catcher

Carry the units through every multiplication and cancel them. If the result does not come out in pesos, the setup is wrong. This single habit catches most mistakes in the set before you ever look at the number.

₱ 100/ton-km × 900 km × 75 tons = ₱ 6,750,000 └── ton and km both cancel, ₱ survives ✓ ₱ 25,000/ton ÷ 4 months = ₱ 6,250/ton-month └── a rate, not a price — now comparable ✓

Watch for rates with compound or scaled units. Problem 11 prices shipping at $1 per kg per 100 km, so a 2,500 km trip is 25 units of “100 km”, not 2,500. Getting that wrong is a factor-of-100 error.

2. Rate normalization — making things comparable

When two alternatives last different lengths of time, produce different amounts, or cover different quantities, divide to get a rate. Then and only then can you compare.

\[ \text{cost per month} = \frac{\text{cost}}{\text{life in months}} \qquad \text{cost per liter} = \frac{\text{cost}}{\text{liters produced}} \]

3. Least common multiple — the other way to do the same thing

Instead of dividing to a rate, scale both alternatives up to a shared horizon: the LCM of their lives. For lives of 4 and 6 months, \(\operatorname{lcm}(4,6)=12\); for 2 and 3 months, \(\operatorname{lcm}(2,3)=6\). Both methods give identical rankings. Use whichever your instructor showed.

4. Weighted sums — bills of materials and machine hours

Extend each line (quantity × unit cost), then total. Nearly half the problem set is this and nothing more. The difficulty is never the sum — it is the unit conversions hidden in the rows.

nails: 100 g at ₱ 300/kg → 0.1 kg × 300 = ₱ 30 milling: 8 min at ₱ 400/hr → (8/60) hr × 400 = ₱ 53.33

5. Ratio comparison — the thirty-second sanity check

For two options where one costs more but delivers more, compare the two ratios directly. If the benefit ratio beats the cost ratio, the pricier option wins — before you compute a single peso.

\[ \frac{p_B}{p_A} \;<\; \frac{L_B}{L_A} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \text{choose } B \]

This is a check on your arithmetic, not a substitute for it. Show the full working, then verify with the ratio.

The decision rule, once and for all

Choose the alternative with the lowest total relevant cost — but only when every alternative produces the same sellable output. When output differs and that output earns a profit, choose the highest total relevant profit instead. Problem 13 is in the set specifically to make you notice the difference.

The whole method

Three rules govern every problem in the set

Rule 1

Only differences matter

Any cost or revenue identical across all alternatives is irrelevant. It cancels. Including it wastes time; excluding it never changes the answer.

This is why Problem 5 hands you “130 tons per day of clinker” and you never use it. Same kiln, same output, same revenue either way.

The flip side: a cost that does differ belongs in the comparison even if it feels unrelated — a fuel penalty, a contractual fine, a start-up cost after a shutdown.

Rule 2

Compare on a common basis

Two numbers measuring different things cannot be compared. Put the alternatives on the same footing first.

Common time — different lives. Convert to cost per month, or scale both to the LCM.

Common output — different production. Convert to cost per liter, per ton, per unit.

Common quantity — a fixed stated requirement. Total cost of meeting exactly that requirement, each way.

Rule 3

Minimize cost only when output is equal

Cost minimization is a shortcut that is valid only when both alternatives deliver the same sellable output.

When one option produces more, and that extra output sells at a profit, the cheaper-per-unit option can still be the wrong choice. Switch the basis from cost to profit.

Problem 13 is exactly this: the ceramic media costs more per liter and is still worth ₱ 351,000 more over six months.

Assignment problem 5 · Pattern E · ME Board Oct 1987

A higher price tag that is actually cheaper

A cement kiln holds about 45 tons of magnesite chrome brick in its burning zone, replaced periodically as the lining wears out.

Given
ItemLocal brickImported brick
Price₱ 25,000 / ton₱ 30,000 / ton
Service life4 months6 months
Brick in burning zone45 tons45 tons
Kiln capacity130 t/day130 t/day

Struck row = identical across alternatives, therefore irrelevant (Rule 1).

The trap

The naive reading is “₱ 25,000 is less than ₱ 30,000, so buy local.” The problem is built to punish exactly this.

The price tag is what it costs to acquire the brick. But what the kiln consumes is brick-months of lining. Local gives four months of service for the money; imported gives six. Those are different amounts of service, so the prices are not comparable numbers.

\[ \text{Equivalent cost} \;=\; \frac{\text{price}}{\text{life}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{\frac{25{,}000}{4} = 6{,}250}_{\text{local, ₱/ton-month}} \;>\; \underbrace{\frac{30{,}000}{6} = 5{,}000}_{\text{imported, ₱/ton-month}} \]

Now both numbers measure the same thing, and imported wins. Multiply by the 45 tons the kiln holds:

\[ \text{Local} = \frac{45 \times 25{,}000}{4} = \frac{1{,}125{,}000}{4} = ₱\,281{,}250\ \text{per month} \] \[ \text{Imported} = \frac{45 \times 30{,}000}{6} = \frac{1{,}350{,}000}{6} = ₱\,225{,}000\ \text{per month} \] \[ \Delta = 281{,}250 - 225{,}000 = \boxed{₱\,56{,}250\ \text{per month}} \]

The one-line sanity check

Imported costs 20% more \(\left(\tfrac{30{,}000}{25{,}000} = 1.20\right)\) but lasts 50% longer \(\left(\tfrac{6}{4} = 1.50\right)\). Since \(1.50 > 1.20\), imported wins before you compute a single peso.

Why the answer is a rate and not a total

The problem never says how long the kiln will run, so there is no fixed total to compute. The honest answer is a rate. If your instructor prefers a total, use the LCM:

\[ \operatorname{lcm}(4,6) = 12\ \text{months} \] \[ \text{Local: } \tfrac{12}{4} = 3 \times 1{,}125{,}000 = ₱\,3{,}375{,}000 \qquad \text{Imported: } \tfrac{12}{6} = 2 \times 1{,}350{,}000 = ₱\,2{,}700{,}000 \] \[ \Delta_{12\text{mo}} = ₱\,675{,}000 \;\div\; 12 = ₱\,56{,}250\ \text{per month} \;\checkmark \]

Assignment problem 6 · Pattern G · ME Board Apr 1988

Ten more men, still late, still worth it

An installation job in its completion stage takes 40 days of 8-hour work with 40 men. The contract expires in 30 days. The contractor adds 10 men. Overtime is forbidden. Liquidated damages run ₱ 2,000 per day of delay; men are paid ₱ 80 per day.

Idea 1 — Work content is conserved

A job holds a fixed amount of work, measured in man-days. Changing the crew changes how fast it gets done, not how much there is.

\[ W = \text{men} \times \text{days} = 40 \times 40 = 1{,}600\ \text{man-days} \qquad (= 12{,}800\ \text{man-hours}) \] \[ t_{50} = \frac{W}{\text{men}} = \frac{1{,}600}{50} = 32\ \text{days} \;>\; 30\ \text{days} \]

So “will he finish on time?” is answered no — two days late. This assumes perfect proportionality: 50 men work exactly 25% faster than 40. Real sites do not behave that way, but board problems always assume it does, and so should you.

The 8-hour day is a quieter distractor. It defines what a day means, but since both options work the same eight hours and overtime is forbidden, it cancels. Work in man-days and never convert.

Idea 2 — Labor cost does not move, and that is the punchline

\[ C_{\text{labor}} = (\text{men} \times \text{days}) \times w = W \times w = 1{,}600 \times 80 = ₱\,128{,}000 \]

Identical for both crews — not a coincidence, just algebra. Work content is locked at 1,600 man-days and the wage is per man-day, so the product cannot change. By Rule 1, labor cost is therefore irrelevant to the decision: you could solve the whole problem from penalties alone and still get ₱ 16,000.

Where this breaks

The invariance holds only because men are paid per day worked. On a flat monthly salary, or with overtime at a premium, labor cost would differ between options and would have to be compared. It is a property of this wage structure, not a general law.

Idea 3 — Liquidated damages are the differential cost

\[ D = \max(0,\; t - 30) \times 2{,}000 \] \[ D_{40} = (40-30)\times 2{,}000 = ₱\,20{,}000 \qquad D_{50} = (32-30)\times 2{,}000 = ₱\,4{,}000 \]
Total cost comparison
Cost component40 men50 men
Duration40 days32 days
Days late (vs 30)102
Labor — 1,600 man-days × ₱ 80128,000128,000
Liquidated damages20,0004,000
Total cost₱ 148,000₱ 132,000

Two questions, two different answers

Will he finish on time? No — 32 days against a 30-day contract.
Does adding men save money? Yes — ₱ 16,000.

Students assume these must match: if the deadline still slips, surely the extra men were pointless. Wrong. The ten men do not erase the penalty, but they cut it from ten days to two, and cost nothing extra to do it. A partial improvement can be economically correct even when it fails to achieve the original goal.

The chart shows something the problem does not ask but worth knowing: 54 men would finish in 29.6 days with zero damages, total ₱ 128,000 — cheaper still. It is not on the menu, since the contractor committed to exactly ten extra. But if your model says more men keep getting cheaper forever, your model is wrong.

The full set

Every problem, approach first

Each one below leads with how to see it — the pattern, and the decision about basis that follows from it — before any arithmetic. That ordering is the skill. The numbers are the easy part.

Problem 1

Door frames — nearby shop or far-off forest

Pattern A · Fixed requirement

How to see it

The requirement is pinned at 75 tons, so the basis is total cost for exactly 75 tons. Two cost components: material, and transport. Transport is quoted per ton-km, so it needs three factors multiplied, not two — that is where people lose it.

Working

Nearby shop material ₱ 2,000/ton × 75 tons = $ 150,000 transport negligible = 0 TOTAL = $ 150,000 Far-off forest material $ 1,250/ton × 75 tons = $ 93,750 transport $ 100/ton-km × 900 km × 75 tons = $6,750,000 TOTAL = $6,843,750

Nearby shop, cheaper by $ 6,693,750.

Watch out

The forest material is 37.5% cheaper — that is the bait. But transport at $100/ton-km over 900 km works out to $90,000 per ton, forty-five times the material price itself. Always compute transport before forming an opinion. Also: your answer key prints $96,750 for 1,250×75; the correct figure is $93,750. Its total of $6,843,750 already uses the right number, so only the middle line is wrong.

Problem 2

Make or buy part No. 010

Pattern B · Avoidable cost

How to see it

Make-or-buy always turns on one question: which costs actually disappear if we stop making it? Direct material, direct labor and variable overhead go away. Fixed overhead — the factory, the supervisors, the depreciation — does not. So it must be charged to the “buy” column too.

Working — short way (differential)

avoidable cost to make = DM 4 + DL 2 + var. OH 2 = ₱ 8/unit purchase price = ₱ 13/unit advantage of making = ₱ 5/unit × 50,000 units = ₱ 250,000

Working — long way (total cost, what the key shows)

Make 50,000 × (4+2+2+4) = ₱ 600,000 Buy 50,000 × 13 = ₱ 650,000 + unavoidable fixed OH = ₱ 200,000 = ₱ 850,000

Make the part, favorable by ₱ 250,000 per year.

Watch out

The Extra Problems file prints Total Unit Cost Php 15, but 4 + 2 + 2 + 4 = 12. Use 12; the answer key does. And never compare ₱ 12 against ₱ 13 and conclude “buy is only ₱ 1 worse” — the ₱ 4 of fixed overhead does not leave when the work does, so buying actually costs ₱ 17 per unit all-in.

Problem 3

Jet engine casting — aluminum or steel

Pattern C · Weighted resource

How to see it

Per-unit comparison with a downstream cost attached. Casting cost is $/kg × kg, machining is given flat, and then a fuel penalty is charged on excess weight. Excess means relative to the lighter option, so the lighter one carries zero penalty by definition.

Working

Aluminum Steel casting $80 × 1.20 = $ 96 $35 × 1.35 = $ 47.25 machining $ 150 $170.00 weight penalty 0 (1.35-1.20) × 1,300 = $195.00 ─────────── ────────────────────── TOTAL / unit $ 246.00 $ 412.25

Aluminum, advantage $ 166.25 per unit.

Watch out

The penalty is charged on the difference of 0.15 kg, not on steel’s full 1.35 kg. Charging the full weight gives $1,755 and a wildly wrong total. Note also that steel’s raw casting cost is half of aluminum’s — the penalty is the only thing that flips the decision, which is the point of including it.

Problem 4

Pampanga Corp — close the plant or keep going

Pattern B · Avoidable cost

How to see it

Both alternatives lose money. That is not a reason to reject either — it means the decision is about which loss is smaller. Contribution margin is already net of variable cost, so it goes straight against fixed overhead. Shutting down does not zero the overhead; it reduces it, and adds start-up costs on the way back.

Working

Stay open (6 months) contribution 4,000 units/mo × 6 mo × ₱ 8 = ₱ 192,000 less fixed overhead = (₱ 320,000) NET RESULT = (₱ 128,000) Shut down (6 months) contribution = ₱ 0 less reduced overhead = (₱ 225,000) less restart / protection costs = (₱ 31,000) NET RESULT = (₱ 256,000)

Stay open. The loss is ₱ 128,000 smaller than shutting down — the lesser evil.

Watch out

The phrase “will definitely result to a loss” is written to push you toward shutting down. Ignore the framing and compare the two numbers. And do not subtract variable cost from the ₱ 8 — contribution margin already has it removed.

Problem 7

Refinery storage tanks — optimum proportions

Pattern F · Optimization

How to see it

Two independent sub-problems that share no arithmetic. Cost: you are told the current design sits at 111% of the minimum, so the minimum is the current cost divided by 1.11. Geometry: hold the volume constant and impose \(H = 4D\), then solve for \(D\). Do them separately; do not try to link them.

Working — volume held constant

original D = 5.2 m, H = 7 m (H/D = 1.35 ✓) V = πD²H/4 = π(5.2)²(7)/4 = 148.66 m³ per tank impose H = 4D: V = π D²(4D)/4 = π D³ D³ = 148.66 / π = 47.32 D = 3.61 m → H = 4D = 14.46 m

Working — cost

current cost is 111% of minimum minimum = 9,000,000 / 1.11 = $ 8,108,108 savings = 9,000,000 - 8,108,108 = $ 891,892

Optimum tank: D ≈ 3.61 m, H ≈ 14.46 m. Expected saving ≈ $ 891,892.

Watch out

“111% of the minimum cost” means current = 1.11 × minimum, so you divide by 1.11. Multiplying by 0.89 instead gives $8,010,000 — wrong by about $98,000. This exact slip is the most common error on the problem.

Problem 8

Suarez Corp — sell now or process further

Pattern D · Incremental

How to see it

The ₱ 30 manufacturing cost is incurred whichever way you go — it is a common cost, and by Rule 1 it cancels. Strip it out and the decision reduces to one comparison: what does the extra processing add to revenue, versus what it adds to cost?

Working

Δ revenue ₱ 55 - ₱ 45 = + ₱ 10 / unit Δ cost additional = - ₱ 15 / unit NET = - ₱ 5 / unit × 30,000 units = - ₱ 150,000 / year check by full margins (₱ 30 included both sides): sell now 45 - 30 = ₱ 15 / unit process more 55 - 30 - 15 = ₱ 10 / unit ✓ same ₱ 5 gap

Sell at the partially processed stage. Advantage ₱ 150,000 per year.

Watch out

The ₱ 55 selling price is designed to look like the better option. Further processing pays only when the price increase exceeds the added cost — here it falls ₱ 5 short. Note that both routes are profitable; you are choosing the more profitable one, not avoiding a loss.

Problem 9

Tapered fastening pin — Design A or B

Pattern C · Weighted resource

How to see it

Everything except lathe and grinder time is stated to be identical, so by Rule 1 only those two lines enter. Multiply hours by each machine’s own rate and sum. Do not compare total hours — the two machines cost different amounts per hour.

Working

per 1,000 units Design A Design B lathe @ $200/hr 16.0 h = $3,200 7.0 h = $1,400 grinder @ $150/hr 4.5 h = $ 675 12.0 h = $1,800 ────────────── ────────────── TOTAL $ 3,875 $ 3,200 (total hours) 20.5 h 19.0 h

Design B, advantage $ 675 per 1,000 units — and since 1,000 units are required per year, $ 675 per year.

Watch out

What B really does is shift work off the expensive lathe onto the cheaper grinder. Even if B had used more total hours, it could still win. Cost-weighted hours decide, never raw hours.

Problem 10

Dining tables — wooden top or granite

Pattern A · Bill of materials

How to see it

A straight bill-of-materials extension: quantity × unit cost on every row, then total. No traps in the logic at all — the entire difficulty is unit conversion hidden in two of the rows.

Working

WOODEN TOP GRANITE TOP wood 0.10 m³ × 12,000 = 1,200 0.15 m³ × 12,000 = 1,800 top 1 × 3,000 = 3,000 1.62 m² × 800 = 1,296 bushes 4 × 10 = 40 4 × 25 = 100 nails 0.100 kg × 300 = 30 0.050 kg × 300 = 15 labour 15 h × 50 = 750 8 h × 50 = 400 ─────── ─────── TOTAL ₱ 5,020 ₱ 3,611

Granite top, cheaper by ₱ 1,409 per table.

Problem 11

Window frames — steel or aluminum

Pattern A · Fixed requirement

How to see it

Landed cost per frame: purchase price plus shipping. Shipping is quoted at $1 per kg per 100 km, so the distance must first be expressed in hundreds of kilometres. That conversion is the entire problem.

Working

2,500 km ÷ 100 = 25 shipping units Steel Aluminum price $ 1,000 $ 1,500 shipping 75 kg × 25 = $1,875 28 kg × 25 = $ 700 ─────── ─────── TOTAL / frame $ 2,875 $ 2,200

Aluminum frames, advantage $ 675 per frame.

Watch out

Using 2,500 instead of 25 gives $188,500 of shipping on a $1,000 frame — absurd on its face, which is exactly why unit analysis catches it. Aluminum costs 50% more to buy and still wins, because it weighs 37% of what steel weighs.

Problem 12

Process planning — three operation sequences

Pattern C · Weighted resource

How to see it

Times are in minutes, rates are per hour. Convert once — either divide each time by 60, or work the whole sum in minutes and divide the total by 60 at the end. The second is faster and less error-prone.

Working — sum in minute-pesos, divide once

Sequence 1 turning-milling-shaping-drilling (5×200) + (8×400) + (10×350) + (3×300) = 1,000 + 3,200 + 3,500 + 900 = 8,600 ÷ 60 = $ 143.33 (26 min) Sequence 2 turning-milling-drilling (5×200) + (14×400) + (3×300) = 1,000 + 5,600 + 900 = 7,500 ÷ 60 = $ 125.00 (22 min) Sequence 3 all CNC (8×1,000) = 8,000 ÷ 60 = $ 133.33 ( 8 min)

Sequence 2 at $ 125.00 per component — $ 8.33 below CNC and $ 18.33 below Sequence 1.

Watch out

CNC finishes in 8 minutes, a third of Sequence 2’s time, and is still more expensive — $1,000/hr buys that speed. Shortest time is not lowest cost. Sequence 2 also beats Sequence 1 despite longer milling, because dropping the shaping operation saves more than the extra milling costs.

Problem 13

Paint sand mill — glass beads or ceramic media

Pattern E · Unequal lives Rule 3 trap

How to see it

The hardest problem in the set, because two things differ at once: the media replenish on different schedules (2 vs 3 months) and the mill produces at different rates (100 vs 120 L/hr). Use LCM(2,3) = 6 months for the time basis. Then — and this is the part the answer key leaves unfinished — notice that output differs, so Rule 3 says cost per liter is not the basis that decides.

Working — six-month common window

Glass beads Ceramic media load 25 kg 30 kg price ₱ 200/kg ₱ 400/kg cost per replenishment ₱ 5,000 ₱ 12,000 replenishments in 6 mo 3 2 ────────── ────────── 6-month media cost ₱ 15,000 ₱ 24,000 output L/hr × 8 h × 25 d × 6 mo glass 100 × 8 × 25 × 6 = 120,000 L ceramic 120 × 8 × 25 × 6 = 144,000 L media cost per liter glass 15,000 / 120,000 = ₱ 0.1250 /L ceramic 24,000 / 144,000 = ₱ 0.1667 /L ← ceramic LOSES here

Why cost per liter is the wrong basis

Output is not equal, and the extra output sells at ₱ 15 profit per liter. Media cost is a rounding error next to that.

extra output 144,000 - 120,000 = 24,000 L extra profit 24,000 L × ₱ 15/L = ₱ 360,000 extra media cost 24,000 - 15,000 = (₱ 9,000) NET GAIN = ₱ 351,000 per 6 months

Yes — recommend the change to ceramic media. It gains ₱ 351,000 over six months despite costing 33% more per liter in media.

Watch out — two things

The answer key stops at cost per liter and never answers the question. If you stop there you will recommend keeping the glass beads, which is backwards. The problem explicitly gives you ₱ 15 profit per liter precisely so you go the extra step.

The key also writes “25 kgs” in the ceramic row, but the problem says 30 kg. Its ₱ 24,000 figure is correct and requires 30 kg (30 × 400 × 2), so it is a transcription slip, not a calculation error.

Repeatable method

Eight steps that work on any problem in the set

Numbered because they genuinely run in order — screen for relevance before choosing a basis, choose a basis before computing.

  1. Identify the alternatives

    Usually two. Name them plainly — “local brick” and “imported brick”, not “A” and “B”.

  2. List every number given, with units

    Units are where the errors hide. ₱/ton is not ₱/ton-month; $/kg/100 km is not $/kg/km.

  3. Screen for relevance

    For each number ask: does this differ between alternatives? If no, set it aside — and say so in your solution. Naming the distractor earns credit.

  4. Name the pattern, then choose the basis

    Fixed requirement → total cost. Make-or-buy or shutdown → avoidable cost. Different lives → per month or LCM. Different output that sells → total profit, not cost.

  5. Build a comparison table

    One column per alternative, one row per cost component, a total row at the bottom. This is the format your answer key uses throughout, and it makes errors visible.

  6. Compute the difference

    State it with units and direction: “imported is cheaper by ₱ 56,250 per month”, never a bare number.

  7. Answer what was actually asked

    Problem 6 asks two things and Problem 7 asks two things. Answer both, explicitly, in words.

  8. Sanity check

    Run the ratio test. Do the units cancel to pesos? Is the magnitude plausible? Did you use a number that should have cancelled?

Formulas worth memorizing

Consumable item, differing lives — Pattern E

\[ C_{\text{month}} = \frac{\text{unit price} \times \text{quantity}}{\text{life}} \qquad T_{\text{common}} = \operatorname{lcm}(L_A,\, L_B) \] \[ \text{choose the pricier option when}\quad \frac{p_B}{p_A} < \frac{L_B}{L_A} \]

Make or buy — Pattern B

\[ C_{\text{make}} = \text{avoidable unit cost} \times Q \qquad C_{\text{buy}} = \text{price} \times Q + \text{unavoidable fixed} \]

Sell or process further — Pattern D

\[ \text{process further iff}\quad \Delta\text{revenue} > \Delta\text{cost} \]

Fixed work content — Pattern G

\[ W = \text{men} \times \text{days} \;\;\text{(invariant)} \qquad t = \frac{W}{\text{men}} \qquad C_{\text{labor}} = W \times w \;\;\text{(invariant if paid per day)} \] \[ D = \max(0,\; t - t_{\text{allowed}}) \times r \]

Cylindrical vessel — Pattern F

\[ V = \frac{\pi D^2 H}{4} \qquad\text{with}\quad H = kD \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; V = \frac{\pi k D^3}{4} \qquad D = \sqrt[3]{\frac{4V}{\pi k}} \]
